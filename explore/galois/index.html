Field extensions

A bigger field is a vector space over a smaller one

Adjoin √5 to ℚ and every number has the form a + b√5. Adding is vector addition in the basis {1, √5}. Multiplying by a fixed number x is a linear map, so x becomes a matrix. The degree of the extension is the dimension.

Field
x = ·1 + ·√5 + ·∛4
Multiplication by x, as a matrix

Column = where a basis vector goes after multiplying by x. Degree = number of columns.

ProofIn ℚ(√5), det = a² − 5b². If it were 0 with b ≠ 0, then √5 = |a/b| would be rational. So every nonzero x is an invertible matrix, and its inverse matrix gives x⁻¹. That is why the set is a field and not just a ring.

PictureThe swap √5 ↦ −√5 keeps every sum and product intact. It sends φ = (1+√5)/2 to (1−√5)/2, the same swap that exchanges the two 3-dimensional characters of A₅.

Splitting fields

One root does not bring its siblings

The roots of x³ − 2 are α, αω, αω² with α = ∛2 and ω = e2πi/3. Pick a field and see which roots live in it.

Field

ProofTower law: [L:ℚ] = [L:ℚ(α)]·[ℚ(α):ℚ] = 2·3 = 6. The other path gives 3·2, the same number. Degrees multiply along a tower because bases multiply: {1, α, α²} × {1, ω} is a basis of L.

Pictureℚ(α) sits inside the real line, and the other two roots are off it. The splitting field has to leave the line.

The Galois group

Symmetries that cannot tell the roots apart

An automorphism of L = ℚ(α, ω) fixing ℚ is decided by two choices: where α goes and where ω goes. Tap one to apply it to the roots. Tap several to compose.

Does order matter? and

ProofA symmetry fixing ℚ preserves every polynomial equation with rational coefficients. So α must go to a root of x³ − 2 (3 choices) and ω to a root of x² + x + 1 (2 choices). At most 6, and |Gal(L/ℚ)| = [L:ℚ] = 6, so all six exist. The group is S₃.

Pictureτ is complex conjugation, so here the symmetries happen to look like rotations and reflections. That is luck of this example. For most polynomials a Galois automorphism is not a continuous map of ℂ.

4

The fundamental theorem

Every subgroup fixes exactly one field

For a subgroup H, collect the numbers in L that every element of H leaves alone. That set is a field. Tap any node: its partner lights up on the other side. Notice that the pictures are upside down relative to each other.

Solid border: normal subgroup, and its field is Galois over ℚ. Dashed: neither.

TheoremFor a finite Galois extension L/K with group G, the map H ↦ LH is a bijection between subgroups and intermediate fields. It reverses inclusion, [L : LH] = |H|, and H is normal exactly when LH/K is Galois. Then Gal(LH/K) ≅ G/H.

BridgeRead the path ℚ ⊂ ℚ(ω) ⊂ L. It is a radical tower: adjoin √−3, then ∛2. Its partner is the chain S₃ ▷ C₃ ▷ {e}. The first step has group S₃/C₃ ≅ C₂, the second C₃. One radical step, one abelian quotient. This is the link between formulas and groups.

5

Radical extensions

One radical gives one abelian layer

Suppose the field already contains ζ = e2πi/n. Adjoin β = ⁿ√2. The roots of xⁿ − 2 sit evenly on a circle, and every symmetry multiplies β by a power of ζ.

n

ProofLet K contain ζ, and σ fix K. Then σ(β)ⁿ = 2, so σ(β)/β is an n-th root of unity, which lies in K. The map σ ↦ σ(β)/β is injective (σ is decided by β) and multiplicative, because σ fixes the root of unity τ(β)/β. So the group sits inside the cyclic group ⟨ζ⟩.

ProofAdjoining ζ itself: σ(ζ) = ζᵐ, and σ ↦ m embeds the group into (ℤ/n)×, which is abelian. So both kinds of radical step have abelian groups.

PictureEvery symmetry of one radical layer is a rotation of its circle of roots. Rotations commute.

6

Solvable groups

Breaking a group into abelian layers

A radical formula is a tower of abelian steps. By the correspondence, that is a chain of subgroups, each normal in the one before, with abelian quotients. Pick a group.

Group

The quintic chain is stuck at A₅. Here is why no smaller layer exists. A normal subgroup is a union of whole conjugacy classes, includes the identity, and its size must divide 60. These are A₅'s classes, read as rotations of the icosahedron. Try to build one.

All 16 possible unions

ProofOnly 1 and 60 divide 60, so A₅ has no normal subgroup besides {e} and itself. It is simple, and it is not abelian. A chain through A₅ must jump straight to {e} with quotient A₅, which is not abelian. So S₅ is not solvable.

TheoremOver ℚ, a polynomial is solvable by radicals exactly when its Galois group is solvable.

7

Two fifth-degree polynomials

Same degree, different symmetry

Both polynomials have 5 roots. The roots of the first sit on a regular pentagon. The second has no visible pattern, and its symmetry group is the full S₅.

x⁵ − 2x⁵ − x − 1
Degree of one root55
Splitting field degree20120
Galois groupF₂₀ = C₅ ⋊ C₄S₅
ChainF₂₀ ▷ C₅ ▷ {e}S₅ ▷ A₅ ▷ {e}
QuotientsC₄, C₅C₂, A₅
Radical towerℚ ⊂ ℚ(ζ₅) ⊂ ℚ(ζ₅, ⁵√2)none exists
Solvable by radicalsyesno

ProofWhy the group of x⁵ − x − 1 is all of S₅. Uses Dedekind's theorem: factoring mod a prime p gives the cycle type of some element of G, when p does not divide the discriminant (here 2869 = 19·151).

  1. Mod 5 it is x⁵ − x − 1, an Artin–Schreier polynomial, irreducible over 𝔽₅. So the polynomial is irreducible over ℚ and G contains a 5-cycle.
  2. Mod 2: x⁵ + x + 1 = (x² + x + 1)(x³ + x² + 1). So G has an element of cycle type (2)(3). Its cube is a transposition.
  3. A 5-cycle and a transposition generate S₅, because 5 is prime. So G = S₅.

PictureThis polynomial has one real root, so complex conjugation swaps two pairs. Geometry alone shows only a small piece of S₅; the reduction mod primes reveals the rest.

Two technical steps of the full proof are not on this bench. A radical tower need not be Galois over ℚ, so the proof first enlarges it to its Galois closure, which is still a radical tower. Then the Galois group of the polynomial is a quotient of the tower's group, and a quotient of a solvable group is solvable.
